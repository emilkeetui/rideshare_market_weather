\section{Motivation and Research Question}

Hurricanes and tropical storms are increasing in frequency due to climate change and are a major cost associated with the damages of climate. They are generally harmful in aggregate, but they have an ambiguous and uneven effect on firms and consumers, potentially benefiting some firms. Hurricanes trigger acute, localized scarcity: flooded roads, driver withdrawal, and surging demand to evacuate or reach safety, and ridesharing platforms use surge pricing during exactly these moments. During Hurricane Ida (1 September 2021), fares reached about three times the TLC pay floor, while the driver's share of the fare fell from 89\% to 72\% at Uber and to 45\% at Lyft.

We study how every hurricane and tropical storm that struck New York City from 2019 to 2026 affected ridesharing fares, rides taken, surge pricing, and the distribution of welfare between riders, drivers, and platforms. \textbf{Core question:} Does surge efficiently ration scarce rides and pull in supply, or does it extract rents from riders with no good alternative? Because transactions are spread over a wide temporal and geographic area, we can compare places before and after each storm, and places more and less affected by it. The NYC rideshare market is large in terms of transactions, employment, and value, so understanding it has implications for millions of people: in 2026 it was valued at \$5.2 billion, employed over 80,000 drivers, and facilitated hundreds of thousands of daily trips.

\section{Related Literature and Theoretical Framing}

Surge pricing reduces wasteful driver search \citep{castillo2017} and raises rider but lowers driver surplus \citep{castillo2025}; rain shifts taxi demand and labor supply \citep{farber2015,brodeur2018}; platforms hold wage-setting power \citep{dube2020}; and hurricane damage is large \citep{kahn2005,hsiang2014,deryugina2017}. But these literatures study routine, reversible imbalances, where riders have time and alternatives, or aggregate, longer-run costs. None study surge pricing when demand is acutely inelastic (life-safety and evacuation trips), supply is physically blocked (flooded, impassable roads), and anti-price-gouging law may be legally activated: the setting where ``surge equals efficient matching'' may invert.

We analyze storms through platform market power in the rider market and the driver market. When both markets are competitive, platforms offer drivers a share of fares equal to their opportunity cost of driving. Market power in the rider market lets platforms charge a higher fare; wage-setting power over drivers lets them reduce the driver's share and capture a greater percentage of fare increases. With demand elasticity $\varepsilon_D$, driver supply elasticity $\varepsilon_S$ and platform cost $c$, the driver's share is
\begin{equation}
\text{DriverShare} = \frac{\text{driver\_pay}}{\text{base\_passenger\_fare}} = \frac{1 - 1/\varepsilon_D - c/p}{1 + 1/\varepsilon_S}.
\end{equation}
Hurricanes increase platform power in the rider market if they reduce competition from alternative transport (subway closures, walking becoming more dangerous than driving) or if rideshares are especially suited for evacuations. They reduce drivers' market power if drivers' outside employment options close, and increase it if drivers demand higher wages to accept the risk of driving during a hurricane. Where the TLC pay floor binds, the share falls mechanically as fares rise, so share results use trips with a slack floor. The pass-through elasticity of driver share to surge is the key structural object.

\section{Data and Variables}

All data are in hand; Table~\ref{tab:vars} defines the variables. The NYC High Volume For-Hire Vehicle (HVFHV) trip records, which rideshare companies must legally file with the TLC, report for every Uber, Lyft and Via trip from 2019 to 2026 the request, pickup and dropoff times and taxi zones, distance, duration, the fare paid and driver pay net of commission. Lacking a surge multiplier, we measure surge as fare per mile relative to the zone's non-storm baseline for that hour of the week, and aggregate trips to taxi zone $\times$ hour ($z,t$) and origin--destination pair $\times$ hour. Yellow and green taxi records give the taxi share; NOAA HURDAT2 tracks and radar rainfall time each storm $s$; storm surge (SLOSH, a bathtub elevation model and tide gauges), FEMA floodplains and 311 complaints show where water reached; MTA alerts record each bus and subway delay or closure; and cellphone data measure population present and net outflow by zone and hour.

\section{Empirical Strategy}

\textbf{Multi-storm stacked event study.} We pool every storm of the HVFHV era (2019--2026), including Isaias, Elsa, Henri and Ida:
\begin{equation}
Y_{z,s,t} = \alpha_{z,s} + \gamma_{s,t} + \sum_{k \neq -1} \beta_k \cdot \text{FloodExposed}_{z,s} \times \mathbb{1}[t = \text{EventHour}_s + k] + X_{z,t}\,\delta + \varepsilon_{z,s,t}
\end{equation}
The unit is taxi zone $\times$ hour, stacked by storm. $\beta_k$ traces out the dynamic effect $k$ hours from the event hour, and leads capture anticipatory evacuation demand. Outcomes are those listed in Table~\ref{tab:vars}. Stacking avoids staggered-adoption bias in two-way fixed effects \citep{cengiz2019,callaway2021} and tests whether pricing algorithms changed across storms.

\textbf{Triple difference.} A simple DiD cannot separate ``this zone always behaves differently during storms'' from ``this zone is specifically flooded once landfall occurs''; the triple interaction Flooded $\times$ StormPeriod $\times$ PostLandfall isolates the latter.

\textbf{Supply--demand decomposition.} $\text{SurgeProxy} = \beta_1 \cdot \text{DemandProxy} + \beta_2 \cdot \text{SupplyProxy} + \text{FE}$, with the proxies in Table~\ref{tab:vars}. Second-stage test: does surge at $t$ actually raise supply at $t+1$? Within origin--destination pairs, $\log \text{share} = \log \text{pay} - \log \text{fare}$ splits each share effect into fare and pay.

\textbf{Storm-track instrument.} Flooded zones may differ in supply, income or drainage even without a storm, so we instrument flood exposure with the zone's distance from the storm track and the track's angle to the coastline, controlling for elevation, distance to coast and rainfall.

\textbf{Inference and welfare.} Standard errors are clustered by zone, with storm-level wild cluster bootstrap \citep{cameron2008}; storms with and without a governor's emergency declaration (General Business Law \S396-r) are compared. Welfare separates transfers ($\Delta$Fare $\times$ TripVolume, split into $\Delta$DriverPay and $\Delta$PlatformMargin), deadweight loss from unmet demand (a taxi vs.\ HVFHS vs.\ no-trip choice model) and driver risk compensation.

\section{Preliminary Evidence}

In a 106.4-million-cell origin--destination--hour panel (171.4 million trips) merged with radar rainfall, fare and driver pay move one for one in ordinary rain. After more than 50 mm over six hours, fare outpaces pay and the driver share falls 7--8 percentage points. The TLC pay floor bound on about 15\% of Uber and 62\% of Lyft trips in September 2021.

\section{Expected Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
\item The first test of surge-pricing efficiency under acutely inelastic, life-safety demand combined with physically constrained supply.
\item A structural pass-through elasticity (driver pay share vs.\ surge) as the headline estimate, not just a reduced-form price effect, with identification beyond a flood DiD: a storm-track IV and a multi-storm stacked design.
\item For adaptation and sustainable development: evidence on whether algorithmic pricing helps or hinders mobility in extreme weather and who bears the cost, transferable to flood-exposed cities of the Global South wherever trip reporting is mandated, and relevant to TLC pay rules, price-gouging law and transit-suspension decisions.
\end{itemize}
